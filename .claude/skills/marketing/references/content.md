# Content — what to publish, and how to compare honestly

From Marcus Sheridan, *They Ask, You Answer* (rev. ed.).
`docs/marketing/content-plan.md` already applies this; read it first.

---

## The philosophy

One obsession: *what is my customer actually searching, asking, feeling and
fearing?* — and then a duty to answer it better than anyone in the space,
including the questions businesses habitually dodge.

Sheridan's counter-example is **ostrich marketing**: fiberglass pool builders
refusing to mention concrete pools on their websites, reasoning that if they
didn't bring it up, buyers wouldn't know the option existed. Buyers found out
anyway, from competitors, framed badly.

> Consumer ignorance is no longer a viable sales and marketing strategy.

Assume the prospect already knows every alternative, every competitor and every
criticism of you. Write as if that is true, because it is.

## The Big 5

The five subjects that move the needle in every industry, because they are what
people research before buying — and the five most businesses avoid.

1. **Pricing and cost**
2. **Problems** — including your own
3. **Versus and comparisons**
4. **Reviews**
5. **Best in class**

**For Kamoe Bisa, topics 4 and 5 are currently unusable.** Reviews require
customers; there are none, and inventing them is out. "Best in class" lists in a
category with no other Dutch-language entrant would be self-serving theatre.
That leaves 1–3, which is where `content-plan.md` already points.

### 1. Pricing and cost

Sheridan calls a missing price "the F-word of the internet" — the emotion is
frustration, and underneath it, *they know the answer and won't tell me*, which
costs trust immediately. The three standard excuses ("every solution is
different", "competitors will find out", "we'll scare people away") all fail: the
last one amounts to *if I'm honest, people won't buy from me*.

Note this cuts against a temptation on the landing page. The current decision is
that the page's *argument* is not about price (D7) while the closing band still
states €9/€79 — that is consistent with Sheridan. Removing the price entirely
would not be.

Where prices vary, explain the factors rather than hiding the range.

### 2. Problems

> When people buy, they worry more about what might go wrong than about what will
> go right.

Nobody searches "positive reviews". They search "X problems". Answering your own
weaknesses is the highest-trust content you can publish, and you are the only one
who can do it accurately.

Honest problems Kamoe Bisa can own, all already documented:
- the coursebook dialogues are formal and touristy — the register limit
- all audio is TTS, not human narration
- it is a structured A1→B1 climb, not a conversation course
- you must activate what enters practice; nothing is scheduled automatically,
  which is exactly the confusion `/hoe-het-werkt` exists to answer
- one person builds it

Each of those is a genuine reason someone should *not* buy, and saying so is what
makes the rest believable.

### 3. Versus and comparisons

The one with the clearest path here: *Kamoe Bisa vs Duolingo*, *vs Anki*,
*vs Volksuniversiteit*, *vs een privéleraar*. `content-plan.md` §42 already names
these. Sheridan's own comparison article generated hundreds of thousands in
revenue, and it worked because it was genuinely unbiased.

## Disarmament — the technique that makes comparison credible

The objection to a company writing a comparison is that it is biased and everyone
knows it. Sheridan's answer is to say so first, and mean it.

His template, before comparing pool types:

> At River Pools, we only sell fiberglass pools, but the truth of the matter is,
> a fiberglass pool might not be the best fit for you. In fact, a concrete or
> vinyl-liner pool might be the better option.

Four moves: state what you sell; admit it isn't right for everyone; name a case
where the alternative genuinely wins; promise an honest look and then deliver one.

**This is the mechanism behind `positioning.md` §5's rule** — "say this as a gain,
never as a dismissal; attack the gap, never the learner or the tool they chose".
Disarmament is *how* you obey it.

Worked, for Duolingo:

> Duolingo is a genuinely good app, and if you are learning through English it
> will take you a long way. It just doesn't offer Indonesian to Dutch speakers at
> all — so you would be learning your partner's language through your second
> language, and the 173 Dutch words already sitting inside Indonesian would be
> invisible to you the whole way.

Worked, for Anki:

> Anki is the best scheduler there is, and it is free. If you enjoy building
> decks, use it — seriously. The catch is that you can only make a card for a
> word you already know exists. Nobody builds themselves a card for *capek* when
> the textbook taught them *lelah*.

Note what both do: concede something real and checkable, then narrow to the one
thing that is actually different. Neither claims to be better overall, and
neither needs to.

## Assignment selling

Sheridan's sales-side idea: send the content *before* the conversation, so
poor-fit prospects self-select out and good-fit ones arrive informed. The
equivalent here is `/hoe-het-werkt` — a prospective buyer reading how activation
works before signing up either understands the model or discovers it isn't for
them. Both outcomes beat a confused new learner opening an empty session.
