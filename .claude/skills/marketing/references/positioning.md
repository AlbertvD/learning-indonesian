# Positioning — the method

From April Dunford, *Obviously Awesome* (2019). `docs/marketing/positioning.md`
already applies this to Kamoe Bisa; this file is the method, for when you need to
re-derive rather than look up.

**Read `docs/marketing/positioning.md` first.** If your question is "what are we",
it is probably already answered there. Come here when the answer is missing,
looks wrong, or the market has moved.

---

## What positioning is

> Positioning is the act of deliberately defining how you are the best at
> something that a defined market cares a lot about.

Dunford's frame: positioning is **context setting**. Customers meeting something
new gather clues to decide how to think about it. Her example is Joshua Bell —
the best classical violinist in America — playing a subway station for 45 minutes
and earning $32.17. *Even a world-class product, poorly positioned, can fail.*

The failure mode is not "bad positioning", it is **default positioning**: you
never chose, so the frame you started building in is the frame you kept. Two traps:

1. **You're stuck on what you meant to build.** The product drifted; your
   description didn't. Her example: the baker who set out to make cake and
   actually made muffins — same batter, entirely different business.
2. **The market moved and you didn't.** The "diet muffin" that should now be a
   "gluten-free paleo snack".

The tell for both: you spend most of a first conversation explaining what you are
rather than why you're good.

**Never write a positioning statement** (`FOR… IS A… WHICH PROVIDES… UNLIKE…`).
Dunford's objection is that it assumes you already know the answers, so it
launders your current assumptions into a deliverable and reinforces the status
quo. Fill in the components instead.

## The five (plus one) components

Order matters — each depends on the one before. Starting from features produces
positioning that doesn't connect to how customers actually evaluate.

1. **Competitive alternatives** — what customers would use or do if you didn't
   exist. Often not a product: "do nothing", "ask my partner", "a spreadsheet".
   This is the yardstick they use to define "better", so it comes first.
2. **Unique attributes** — capabilities you have that those alternatives lack.
   Features, not benefits. List generously, including ones the team thinks are
   negatives. "Great customer service" and "easy to use" are not attributes —
   they're unproven values. Ask what makes them true and provable.
3. **Value (and proof)** — what those attributes let the customer *achieve*.
   Your opinion of your value is not proof.
4. **Target market characteristics** — the identifiable traits that make some
   buyers care a lot. Not demographics. "The customers who buy quickly, rarely
   ask for discounts, and tell their friends."
5. **Market category** — the frame that makes your value obvious. Declaring a
   category triggers assumptions about competitors, features and price. Choose
   one where the assumptions work for you.
6. **(Bonus) Relevant trend** — why this matters *right now*. Optional, and
   dangerous: a trend without a category makes you cool but baffling ("the
   sharing economy for pets"). Never layer a trend for its own sake.

## The 10 steps

1. Understand the customers who love your product
2. Form a positioning team
3. Align vocabulary and let go of positioning baggage
4. List true competitive alternatives
5. Isolate unique attributes
6. Map attributes to value themes
7. Determine who cares a lot
8. Find the market frame of reference
9. Layer on a trend (carefully)
10. Capture the positioning so it can be shared

⚠️ **Steps 1 and 2 were not properly done for Kamoe Bisa, and `positioning.md` §7
says so.** Step 1 assumes enough happy customers to see a pattern; there are
none. Step 2 wants a cross-functional team precisely because one person's view of
their own product is unreliable; this was the owner plus an agent.

Dunford's own guidance for that situation: **keep the positioning loose.** "Think
of your product as a fishing net… position it broadly until you have enough
customer experience to see patterns." The first ten paying customers either
confirm the current position or destroy it, and `positioning.md` gets rewritten
from what they say. Do not write copy that hardens a guess into a fact.

## The three styles

Choosing a category is not enough — you also choose how you intend to win in it.

**Head to Head** — win an existing category outright. Rarely right for a small
company: "trying to out-cola Coke". Viable only where a category exists but no
clear leader has emerged.

**Big Fish, Small Pond** — dominate a subsegment whose needs the leader doesn't
meet. Requires: the category is well understood, there is a clear leader that
isn't you, the subsegment has a specific unmet need, and it is *identifiable* —
you could build a list. Her example: Janna Systems repositioning from "enterprise
CRM" (competing with Siebel, described by a prospect as "a cheaper, crappier
Siebel") to "CRM for investment banks" — revenue $2M → $70M in eighteen months.

**Create a New Game** — define a new category. Only when every existing category
would hide your differentiators, and you have the resources to teach a market
that the category should exist.

**Kamoe Bisa sits in Big Fish, Small Pond**, and the fit is unusually clean:
`positioning.md` §5 chose *"de app voor Nederlanders met een Indonesische band"*
over the broader "Indonesian language course". The subsegment is identifiable
(Dutch speakers with an Indonesian tie), the unmet need is specific (nobody
serves this pair in Dutch), and the advantage is structural rather than a feature
— an English-routed course *cannot* use Dutch loanwords, whatever it spends.

⚠️ Dunford's warning applies: the subsegment must be big enough to hit the
business goal. The heritage segment is sized (~1.5–2M, market research §2). The
partner segment — now primary — **is not sized**. `personas.md` flags this. Size
it before spending on channels aimed at it.

⚠️ And re-check the Duolingo asymmetry before any campaign. Duolingo added ~148
AI-generated courses in 2025 and could add Indonesian-for-Dutch at any time. If
they do, the argument shifts from "does not exist" to "hand-authored grammar,
spreektaal and a loanword bridge versus an AI-generated course" — still good, but
different, and it must be made honestly.

## Applying it

When positioning work produces something new, **write it into
`docs/marketing/positioning.md`**, then let the surface quote it. A position that
lives only in one page's copy is a position the next page will contradict.
