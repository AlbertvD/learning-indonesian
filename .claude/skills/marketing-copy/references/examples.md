# Examples — what good actually looks like

Rules are easy to agree with and hard to apply. These are the examples that make
the rules operational. Read this file when you are stuck, and steal the *shape*
rather than the words.

---

## 1. The classics, and precisely why each works

**Rolls-Royce** (Ogilvy)
> "At 60 miles an hour, the loudest noise in this new Rolls-Royce comes from the
> electric clock."

The most-cited headline in advertising, and the lesson is not "be clever" — it is
**concrete beats superlative**. He could have written "supremely quiet". Instead
he gave you a fact you can picture and *check*. The claim is made by the reader,
not the writer. That is Heath's Concreteness and Credibility in one sentence.

**Life Savers**
> "Please don't lick this page."

Unexpectedness. It violates what an ad is allowed to say, so you read it. Note it
sells nothing directly and still communicates flavour better than "delicious".

**Trader Joe's**, on who its customer is (Heath)
> "an unemployed college professor who drives a very, very used Volvo"

The alternative was *"high socioeconomic status, quality-conscious but also
budget-conscious, values variety and new experiences."* Both describe the same
person; only one puts the same picture in every employee's head. **When you find
yourself writing adjectives, reach for a person instead.**

**Reagan, 1980** (Heath, on Credibility)
> "Before you vote, ask yourself if you are better off today than you were four
> years ago."

He had every economic statistic available and used none of them. The technique is
**testable credibility** — hand the reader a way to verify the claim inside their
own head. Far stronger than a number they must take on trust, and *available to a
product with no reviews and no track record*, which is this one exactly.

**StoryBrand's photographer**
He removed 90% of the text from his sales page and stopped writing "f-stop" and
"depth of field". He replaced them with:
> "Take those great pictures where the background is blurry."

This is the Curse of Knowledge cured in one line, and it is the closest analogue
to this project's own problem. "FSRS scheduling on capabilities" is *f-stop*.
"Words come back just before you forget them" is *the blurry background*.

**FedEx's Purple Promise** (Heath, on Story)
Not "we are committed to reliability" but: a driver and a ramp agent, blocked by a
crashed tractor-trailer, carrying every package the last mile to the airport on
foot. **A policy is forgettable; a person doing something specific is not.**

## 2. Worked rewrites from this project

These are real. Each shows the principle, the line as written, and the line as
fixed — including two that were wrong on the facts, because in this product the
craft failure and the truth failure keep turning out to be the same failure.

### 2a. The reader never appears — StoryBrand

**Written:**
> Ik probeerde het met Anki en Duolingo. Ik kreeg het niet te pakken.
> Wat ontbrak was onderdompeling. Ik heb er vijf dingen voor op mijn server
> gezet… Ik wilde dit omdat aan tafel iedereen overging op Engels…

Four sentences, four "ik", no "jij". True, honest, and the reader is watching
someone else's home movie.

**The fix is not to delete the founder story** — it is the guide earning empathy
and authority — **it is to turn.** Structurally:

> *[reader's situation]* → *"Ik ook, jarenlang."* → *[what I tried, what failed]*
> → *"Dus heb ik gebouwd wat ik zocht…"* → *"…zodat jij aan tafel iets terug kunt
> zeggen."*

The last clause is the whole repair: the story ends **in the reader's life**, not
in the builder's.

### 2b. Abstraction where a photograph belongs — Concreteness

| Written | Fixed |
|---|---|
| "Wat ontbrak was onderdompeling." | "Een reader zonder teksten. Een luisterbibliotheek zonder audio." |

*Onderdompeling* is a concept. *An empty reader* is a thing you can see. The
second version also does the argument's work — it shows why the stack failed
instead of asserting it.

### 2c. The best line in the wrong place — Sugarman

The strongest sentence written for this page was, for a while, in band 2:
> **"Ik heb ze opgezet. Ik heb ze nooit gevuld."**

Short, concrete, surprising, self-deprecating, and impossible to write about any
other product. It was sitting two screens below a headline that merely
*summarised*. **Find your best line and check whether it is buried. It usually is.**

### 2d. Disarmament — how to name a competitor

The pattern, from Sheridan (see the `marketing` skill's `references/content.md`),
executed here:

> "Duolingo is een goede app, en wie via het Engels leert komt er ver mee.
> Alleen: Indonesisch wordt aan Nederlandstaligen helemaal niet aangeboden."

> "En Anki? Dat zou lelah prima voor je inplannen. **Alleen had jij die kaart
> moeten maken. Die voor capek was nooit in je opgekomen.**"

Concede something real and checkable first, *then* narrow to the one thing that
is actually different. The Anki line is the best on the page because the concession
is genuine, the criticism is narrow and true, and it lands as an image rather than
a claim.

### 2e. Two failures no rule caught — and why they belong in a craft file

**A sentence that contradicted itself:**
> "…aan tafel schakelde iedereen over op Nederlands, uit beleefdheid, **en ik het
> net niet kon volgen**."

If they switched to *your* language, following is not the problem. Fluent, plausible,
incoherent — and it survived the honesty gate, the tests, a subagent review (which
quoted it approvingly as "the best line on the page"), and several read-throughs.
The owner caught it in one reading.
**Fixed** by repairing the consequence, not the premise: the courtesy *is* the
injury — *"je bent wel degene voor wie de tafel zich aanpast."*

**A claim that oversold by implication:**
> "De teksten staan erin, op niveau."

True. Also: 13 texts, of which **2 at B1 and 1 at B2**. A B1 reader hears "there is
a levelled library for me" and finds two. **Fixed** by stating the real numbers and
naming the limit — *"De verhalenbibliotheek is nog klein en groeit; het zwaartepunt
ligt nu bij A1 en A2."*

The lesson for this file: **catchiness and honesty fail in the same place.** Both
of those defects came from writing a sentence that sounded right instead of
checking what it meant. The cure for both is the same — be concrete enough that
the sentence can be checked.

## 3. Headline patterns that fit this product's awareness state

The reader is **solution-aware, not product-aware** (see `awareness.md`): they
know they want the language and that apps exist; they do not know a
Dutch-language option exists at all. So headlines lead with desire or problem,
never with the product name.

| Pattern | Shape | For this product |
|---|---|---|
| **Confession** | An admission that costs the writer something | "Ik heb ze opgezet. Ik heb ze nooit gevuld." |
| **Recognition** | A situation the reader knows is theirs | "Aan tafel schakelt iedereen over. Uit beleefdheid." |
| **The specific contrast** | Two things and the gap between them | "Duolingo leert je *lelah*. Je schoonmoeder zegt *capek*." |
| **Unexpected number** | A count that reframes | "Je kent al 173 Indonesische woorden." |
| **The false assumption** | Name what they wrongly believe | "Je begint niet bij nul." |

Weak patterns, and why:
- **Product name first** — a most-aware move, wasted on a reader who has never
  heard of you.
- **A benefit with no specificity** — "Leer Indonesisch dat blijft hangen" is a
  claim any app could make, which is why it was replaced.
- **A question the reader can answer "no" to** — "Wil je Indonesisch leren?" ends
  the conversation for everyone who is browsing.

---

## 4. A note on stealing

Steal shapes, never sentences. "At 60 miles an hour…" works because it is true of
a Rolls-Royce; an imitation that is not true of this product is worse than a dull
line, because it will be checked. Every example above earns its effect from a
fact — a clock, a Volvo, an empty reader, two words at a kitchen table. **Find the
fact first. The sentence is the easy part.**
