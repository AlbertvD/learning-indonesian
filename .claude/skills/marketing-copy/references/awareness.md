# Awareness — how much does the reader already know?

Eugene Schwartz, *Breakthrough Advertising*. Book in `~/Downloads`.

The oldest and least intuitive of the five, and the one that decides **what a
headline is allowed to do**. Schwartz's core claim: you cannot create desire, you
can only channel desire that already exists — so the writer's job is to find where
the reader already is and meet them there.

---

## The five states of awareness

Quoting his framing: *"The more aware your market, the easier the selling job, the
less you need to say."*

**1. Most aware** — knows the product, knows what it does, wants it, has not got
round to buying. The headline needs little more than the name and the price.
Schwartz is witheringly clear that this requires no creativity at all.

**2. Product-aware** — knows the product exists but is not yet convinced. The
headline names the product and asserts its superiority; the body documents it.

**3. Solution-aware** — knows the *kind* of solution exists and wants the result,
but does not know your product. **Lead with the desire or the result; introduce the
product as the mechanism, after.**

**4. Problem-aware** — feels the problem, does not know a solution exists. Lead with
the problem, sharply. Naming the product early is wasted.

**5. Completely unaware** — has not identified the problem, or is indifferent, or
resistant. The hardest. Schwartz is explicit, and it is the most counter-intuitive
thing in the book:

> At this stage of your market, a direct statement of what your product does, what
> desire it satisfies, or what problem it solves, simply will not work… You cannot
> mention them in your headline.

For that market you lead with identification, story, or a startling fact — and
arrive at the product much later.

## Where Kamoe Bisa's reader actually sits

**Robin is solution-aware (state 3), edging toward problem-aware (state 4) on the
specific point that makes this product exist.**

Working it through:
- He knows he wants to speak his partner's language. **The desire pre-exists** —
  Schwartz would say this is the easy part, and it is why the founder story lands.
- He knows language apps and courses exist; he has tried Duolingo (`personas.md` §1).
- **He does not know a Dutch-language Indonesian course exists** — and per
  `positioning.md` §1's evidence box, for the big platforms it does not. So on
  *that* point he is state 4 or even 5: he does not know the gap is a gap.
- He has never heard of this product. Not state 1 or 2.

**What follows, concretely:**

| Do | Do not |
|---|---|
| Lead with the desire (speaking at the table) or the problem (nothing serves NL→ID) | Lead with the product name — a state-1 move, wasted here |
| Introduce the product as the *mechanism* that satisfies the existing desire | Assert superiority before the reader knows the category exists |
| Spend a line establishing that a Dutch-language option is even possible | Assume they know what makes this different — they do not know it is different |
| Let the eyebrow carry the category so the headline can carry the tension | Make the headline do category-identification work |

This is also *why* the loanword bridge works so well: **"je kent er al 173"** is a
state-4/5 device. It does not describe the product at all. It reframes what the
reader believes about themselves, and the product becomes relevant afterwards.

⚠️ Note the two personas sit at different states, which is the real argument for
the doors band. Sanne, the stalled app-hopper, is state 3 and searching — she
would respond to a mechanism claim. Robin needs the desire first. The page
currently serves Robin in the hero and Sanne in a door, which is the right
ordering given the persona decision.

## Sophistication: how many claims has this market already heard?

Schwartz's second ladder, and it runs in parallel. Roughly:

1. **First to market** — state the claim plainly. It is new, so it works.
2. **Claim has been made before** — enlarge or outdo it.
3. **Claims are exhausted** — **name the mechanism.** Stop claiming *what* and
   start explaining *how*.
4. **Mechanism claims are exhausted** — elaborate the mechanism.
5. **Everything is exhausted** — abandon claims; go to identification and story.

**Language learning as a category is at stage 4–5.** Every learner has been
promised fluency, promised it is fun, promised ten minutes a day. Direct benefit
claims are dead on arrival — which is exactly why *"Leer Indonesisch dat blijft
hangen"* read as generic. It is a stage-1 headline in a stage-5 market.

**But NL→ID as a category is at stage 1**, because nothing else serves it. That is
the asymmetry worth exploiting: within the crowded category the product must lead
with mechanism, identification and story; on the *pair* it can still make a plain
claim, because nobody has made it.

That is the strategic reason the loanword line and the register pair work while
"words that stick" does not — they are stage-1 claims available in a stage-5
market, because they belong to a category of one.

## Using it

Two questions before writing any headline:

1. **What does this reader already know and want?** Write the headline for that
   state, not for the state you wish they were in.
2. **How many times has this reader heard this claim?** If the answer is "many",
   do not make the claim — show the mechanism, or tell the story instead.

Schwartz's underlying discipline, which survives all the 1960s specifics: *the
headline's job is to connect an existing desire to your mechanism.* Not to
announce, not to summarise, not to be clever.
