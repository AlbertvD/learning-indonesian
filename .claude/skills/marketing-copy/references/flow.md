# Flow — will they read the second sentence?

Joseph Sugarman, *The Adweek Copywriting Handbook*. Book in `~/Downloads`.

Miller decides whose story it is. Heath decides whether it is remembered. Sugarman
is the only one of the five who is about **the mechanics of the sentence you are
writing right now**.

---

## The axioms that matter here

Sugarman has fifteen; these are the ones that apply to a landing page. Quoted.

> **Axiom 2:** All the elements in an advertisement are primarily designed to do
> one thing and one thing only: get you to read the first sentence of the copy.

> **Axiom 3:** The sole purpose of the first sentence in an advertisement is to
> get you to read the second sentence.

> **Axiom 6:** Your readers should be so compelled to read your copy that they
> cannot stop reading until they read all of it as if sliding down a slippery
> slide.

> **Axiom 8:** Keep the copy interesting and the reader interested through the
> power of curiosity.

> **Axiom 9:** Never sell a product or service. Always sell a concept.

> **Axiom 11:** Copy should be long enough to cause the reader to take the action
> you request.

> **Axiom 12:** Every communication should be a personal one, from the writer to
> the recipient, regardless of the medium used.

> **Axiom 13:** The ideas presented in your copy should flow in a logical fashion,
> anticipating your prospect's questions and answering them as if the questions
> were asked face-to-face.

> **Axiom 14:** In the editing process, you refine your copy to express exactly
> what you want to express with the fewest words.

## The slippery slide, practically

The headline's job is not to summarise. It is to make the next line unavoidable.
Then that line does the same, all the way down.

**How it is built:**

1. **Make the first sentence almost impossible not to finish.** Short. Sugarman
   goes as far as one or two words. A long, complete, self-contained first
   sentence gives the reader a natural place to stop, which is exactly what you
   do not want.
2. **Open a gap and delay closing it.** State something that raises a question,
   answer it a beat later. *"Ik heb ze opgezet. Ik heb ze nooit gevuld."* — the
   second sentence creates the question ("why not?") that pulls you into the third.
3. **Anticipate the objection at the moment it forms** (Axiom 13). The reader
   thinking *"but Anki already does this"* should meet the Anki paragraph right
   then — not three bands later.
4. **Get agreement early** (Axiom 5). Open with things the reader knows are true
   of themselves. Every silent "yes" makes the next sentence easier to accept.

**How it breaks, on this page:**
- Headlines that *summarise* rather than provoke. A statement of completed fact —
  "Ik probeerde het met Anki en Duolingo" — closes rather than opens.
- 34-word sentences with five clauses. Every clause boundary is an exit.
- Bands that each restate the thesis. If a band could be deleted without loss,
  the reader has already sensed that and left.

## "Never sell a product, always sell a concept"

Axiom 9 is the most useful single line in the book for this product.

The product is *a Dutch-language Indonesian course with FSRS scheduling*. That is
the product. **The concept is: the stack you would otherwise build, already
assembled and already filled.** Nobody wants a course; they want to stop being the
person the table adjusts for.

This is also the same instruction `positioning.md` §1 gives from the strategy side
— sell **assembly**, never a feature list. When two independent traditions agree,
treat it as settled.

## On length

Axiom 11 refuses the "nobody reads long copy" reflex: copy should be **long enough
to cause the action**, no shorter and no longer. Ogilvy agrees; both were writing
for readers who had to be earned.

The nuance for a landing page: length is fine, but each band must **advance** the
argument. This page's eight bands are defensible only if each one does a job the
others do not. Two bands making the same point is not "long copy", it is repetition,
and the reader leaves at the repeat rather than at the length.

## The editing pass (Axiom 14)

Sugarman edits toward *exactly what you want to say, in the fewest words*. Three
mechanical moves that work on Dutch as well as English:

1. **Cut the throat-clearing.** *"Wat ontbrak was onderdompeling"* → *"Er ontbrak
   onderdompeling"*, or better, replace the abstraction entirely.
2. **Break the long ones.** Any sentence over ~25 words, look for the natural full
   stop. The rhythm of *short. short. longer explanatory sentence.* is what makes
   a slide feel steep.
3. **Delete every word that survives its own deletion.** If removing it changes
   nothing, it was scaffolding.

## Where Sugarman does not apply

He is writing long-form direct response — mail-order ads with a coupon, where the
reader either buys or does not, and there is no relationship afterwards. Two
adjustments:

- **His curiosity techniques can shade into manipulation.** Opening a gap you close
  honestly is craft. Opening one you close with something else is a trick, and this
  product's whole position is built on being checkable.
- **His urgency devices do not transfer.** No invented deadlines, no false scarcity.
  The `marketing` skill's honesty gate outranks every axiom here.
