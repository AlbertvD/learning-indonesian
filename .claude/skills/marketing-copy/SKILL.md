# Marketing copy — the craft layer

The `marketing` skill decides **what is true and worth saying**. This one decides
**how it gets said.** Load both. Strategy first, then this.

They were split on 2026-08-17 for a reason worth remembering: the strategy skill
was built from four books — Dunford, Sheridan, Weinberg & Mares, Ramanujam — and
**not one of them is about writing a sentence.** The copy that came out obeyed
every rule and read flat. The owner's verdict was *"not really catchy."* He was
right, and the gap was structural: rules about *what* to say, nothing about *how*.

---

## Start here: the one diagnosis that catches most flat copy

**Read the draft and count the "you"s.**

If the copy is mostly *we/I/our product*, the reader is a spectator at someone
else's story, and no amount of polish will fix it. This is the single most common
defect and the fastest to spot.

The lived example, from this project's own landing page:

> *Ik probeerde het met Anki en Duolingo. Ik kreeg het niet te pakken… Ik heb er
> vijf dingen voor op mijn server gezet… Ik wilde dit omdat…*

Every sentence true, every rule obeyed, and the reader never appears. Miller's
correction: **the customer is the hero; the brand is the guide.** A founder story
is not the hero's story — it is how the guide earns two credentials, *empathy*
("I have been where you are") and *authority* ("and I found the way through") —
and then it **turns to the reader.** A founder story that never turns is a memoir.

Second-fastest check: **is the strongest line buried?** Scan for the most concrete,
most surprising sentence in the draft. If it is in paragraph four, it is in the
wrong place. On this project's page the best line — *"Ik heb ze opgezet. Ik heb ze
nooit gevuld."* — was two bands below the hero.

## The five lenses

Each answers a different question. Load the reference when you are doing that
specific job; the summary here is enough for a quick pass.

| Question | Lens | Reference |
|---|---|---|
| Whose story is this, and does it turn to the reader? | **StoryBrand** (Miller) | `references/storybrand.md` |
| Will anyone remember or repeat it? | **Made to Stick** (Heath) | `references/stickiness.md` |
| Will they read the second sentence? | **Sugarman** | `references/flow.md` |
| How much do they already know? | **Schwartz** | `references/awareness.md` |
| What does a good one look like? | worked examples | `references/examples.md` |

**`references/examples.md` is the one to read if you only read one.** Rules are
easy to nod at and hard to apply; the examples are what actually transfer.

### StoryBrand, in one line
A character wants something → hits a problem → meets a guide → who gives a plan →
and calls them to action → helping them avoid failure and reach success. Your
brand is **never** the character. It is the guide.
**The grunt test:** could someone look at the page for five seconds and grunt back
what you offer, why they want it, and what to do next? If not, nothing else matters.

### Made to Stick, in one line
**S**imple · **U**nexpected · **C**oncrete · **C**redible · **E**motional ·
**S**tory. Use it as a checklist on a finished draft, not a template for a blank
page. The villain it names — the **Curse of Knowledge** — is the reason a builder
writes "FSRS scheduling on capabilities" instead of "words come back just before
you forget them." *You cannot unknow what you know; you can only translate it.*

### Sugarman, in one line
Every element exists to get the first sentence read; the first sentence exists
only to get the second read. Copy should be a **slippery slide** — the reader
cannot stop. Hence: short first sentences. Open curiosity gaps and close them
late. And *"never sell a product, always sell a concept."*

### Schwartz, in one line
Match the headline to how aware the reader already is. **Kamoe Bisa's reader is
solution-aware but not product-aware** — they know they want to learn Indonesian
and know apps exist; they do not know a Dutch-language option exists at all. So
the headline leads with the *desire or the problem*, never with the product name,
and the mechanism comes after. Naming the product in the headline is a
most-aware move, and would waste it here. Full ladder in the reference.

## Before you call a draft done

Not a substitute for the `marketing` skill's honesty gate — run that too. This
one is about whether the writing works.

1. **Count the "you"s.** Does it turn to the reader, and does it turn *before*
   the CTA?
2. **Grunt test.** Five seconds: what is it, why do I want it, what do I do?
3. **Is the best line first?** Find the most concrete sentence; move it up.
4. **Read the first sentence alone.** Does it make the second one necessary?
5. **Concrete or abstract?** Circle every abstraction and try to replace it with
   something you could photograph. "Onderdompeling" is abstract; "een reader
   zonder teksten" is a photograph.
6. **Curse of Knowledge sweep.** Any word that means something specific to the
   builder and something vague to everyone else — FSRS, capabilities, projection,
   activation — is a defect until translated.
7. **Read it aloud.** Anything you stumble on gets rewritten. This catches more
   Dutch-that-was-thought-in-English than any rule.
8. **Cut 20%.** Sugarman: edit to say exactly what you mean in the fewest words.
   The 20% you cut is almost always throat-clearing at the start of sentences.

## The Dutch problem, specifically

The product is Dutch-first (`marketing` skill, `positioning.md` §8), and the
recurring failure is copy **thought in English and rendered into Dutch**. It
passes every rule and reads like a translation.

The tell: compare the NL and EN versions of the same line. **If the English is
the better sentence, the Dutch is a calque** and needs rewriting from scratch in
Dutch — not editing.

Observed on this page, and worth keeping as calibration:

| Written | Problem | Better |
|---|---|---|
| *"Hier zit dat in één sessie per dag: grammatica, verhalen…, een affixtrainer, een werkplaats… — en een planner die…"* | 34 words, five clauses, em-dash tail. The most AI-shaped sentence on the page | Three items, one clause each |
| *"Het werkt ook."* | Ambiguous — reads "it also works". The EN *"It works, too"* is clear | *"Dat werkt ook echt."* |
| *"en het is met opzet zo"* | Stiff | *"en dat is expres"* |
| *"dat antwoord staat er dan nog"* | Staat *waar*? Opaque | *"het antwoord staat dan nog in je werkgeheugen"* |
| *"Drie eeuwen gedeelde geschiedenis liet honderden woorden achter"* | English word order carried over | *"Driehonderd jaar samen levert honderden woorden op die nog in het Indonesisch zitten"* |

Sentences from the same page that are genuinely Dutch, as a target to aim at:
*"Die voor capek was nooit in je opgekomen."* · *"Antwoorden op vragen die je nog
niet kon stellen."* · *"Dat is precies het punt."* · *"Ik heb ze opgezet. Ik heb
ze nooit gevuld."*

Note what those four have in common: short, concrete, and none of them could be
a sentence about any other product.

## Where craft must yield

This skill makes copy persuasive. Persuasion is exactly what makes a false claim
dangerous, so three limits are absolute:

- **The `marketing` skill's honesty gate wins every time.** Zero customers means
  no invented proof; all audio is TTS; no efficacy numbers; verified counts only;
  the register limit binds every promise. A catchier sentence that breaks one of
  those is not an improvement, it is a liability.
- **Curiosity gaps must close honestly.** Sugarman's technique is to open a gap
  and answer it. Opening one you never close, or closing it with something other
  than what was implied, is a bait-and-switch and it burns the trust the founder
  story exists to build.
- **Emotion must be true.** Heath's "emotional" means *make them feel what is
  actually there* — the gap at the family table is real. Manufactured urgency,
  invented scarcity and fake deadlines are a different trade, and not this one.

One more, specific to this product: the founder story is **biography**. Nothing in
this skill authorises improving it. If a detail would land better untrue, the
answer is to ask the owner for a better true detail — that is exactly how "five
apps open" became "five services stood up and never filled", which was both truer
and far better copy.
