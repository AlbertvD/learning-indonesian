# Marketing copy — the craft layer

The `marketing` skill decides **what is true and worth saying**. This one decides
**how it gets said.** Load both. Strategy first, then this.

Why they are separate: the strategy skill is built from four books about
positioning, publishing, channels and pricing, and **not one of them is about
writing a sentence.** Copy written from strategy alone obeys every rule and reads
flat.

> **This skill holds technique, not content.** It does not know the product, the
> audience, or which claims are available — the project's own docs do, and they
> outrank this file. Never write a specific product's sentences back into this
> skill as rules: an example illustrating a technique is fine, "use this line" is
> a content decision made on the owner's behalf, and it will regenerate every
> time someone edits the copy.

## Start here: the one diagnosis that catches most flat copy

**Read the draft and count the "you"s.**

If the copy is mostly *we/I/our product*, the reader is a spectator at someone
else's story, and no amount of polish will fix it. This is the most common defect
and the fastest to spot.

Miller's correction: **the customer is the hero; the brand is the guide.** A
founder story is not the hero's story — it is how the guide earns two
credentials, *empathy* ("I have been where you are") and *authority* ("and I
found the way through") — and then it **turns to the reader.** A founder story
that never turns is a memoir.

Second-fastest check: **is the strongest line buried?** Find the most concrete,
most surprising sentence in the draft. If it is in paragraph four, it is in the
wrong place.

## The five lenses

Each answers a different question. Load the reference when you are doing that

**`references/examples.md` is the one to read if you only read one.** Rules are
easy to nod at and hard to apply; examples are what transfer.

### StoryBrand, in one line
A character wants something → hits a problem → meets a guide → who gives a plan →
and calls them to action → helping them avoid failure and reach success. Your
brand is **never** the character. It is the guide.
**The grunt test:** could someone look at the page for five seconds and grunt back
what you offer, why they want it, and what to do next? If not, nothing else matters.

### Made to Stick, in one line
**S**imple · **U**nexpected · **C**oncrete · **C**redible · **E**motional ·
**S**tory. Use it as a checklist on a finished draft, not a template for a blank
page. The villain it names — the **Curse of Knowledge** — is why a builder writes
the internal name for a mechanism instead of what the user gets from it. *You
cannot unknow what you know; you can only translate it.*

### Sugarman, in one line
Every element exists to get the first sentence read; the first sentence exists
only to get the second read. Copy should be a **slippery slide** — the reader
cannot stop. Hence: short first sentences. Open curiosity gaps and close them
late. And *"never sell a product, always sell a concept."*

### Schwartz, in one line
Match the headline to how aware the reader already is. A reader who does not know
your category exists cannot be sold on your product name; lead with the desire or
the problem and introduce the product as the mechanism, after. Naming the product
in the headline is a most-aware move and is wasted on anyone else. Full ladder in
the reference.

## Before you call a draft done

Not a substitute for the `marketing` skill's claim rules — run those too. This one
is about whether the writing works.

1. **Count the "you"s.** Does it turn to the reader, and *before* the CTA?
2. **Grunt test.** Five seconds: what is it, why do I want it, what do I do?
3. **Is the best line first?** Find the most concrete sentence; move it up.
4. **Read the first sentence alone.** Does it make the second one necessary?
5. **Concrete or abstract?** Circle every abstraction and try to replace it with
   something you could photograph.
6. **Curse of Knowledge sweep.** Any word that means something precise to the
   builder and something vague to everyone else is a defect until translated.
7. **Read it aloud.** Anything you stumble on gets rewritten. This catches more
   than any rule — especially copy that was thought in one language and rendered
   into another.
8. **Cut 20%.** Sugarman: say exactly what you mean in the fewest words. The 20%
   you cut is almost always throat-clearing at the start of sentences.

## Writing in a language that is not the default

When a product's audience is not English-speaking, the recurring failure is copy
**thought in English and rendered into the target language**. It passes every
rule and reads like a translation.

The tell: compare the two versions of the same line. **If the English is the
better sentence, the other one is a calque** and needs rewriting from scratch in
that language — not editing.

What calques look like, in any language: source-language word order carried over;
an idiom translated literally so it means something adjacent; 30-word sentences
with an em-dash tail, which is a shape almost no one writes by hand; and a
register that is stiffer than a native speaker would use for the same thought.

The target to aim at: sentences that are short, concrete, and could not be said
about any other product.

## Confidence is not the enemy of honesty

The common failure in careful copy is not overclaiming — it is **hedging**.
Disclaimers accumulate, each one individually defensible, and together they read
as someone who insists on telling the truth about everything.

The distinction: **do not make false claims** is a rule about content. **Do not
keep announcing that you are not making them** is a rule about voice. A confident
writer simply does not write the false sentence; they do not append a note
explaining that they did not.

So: write the strongest true sentence available. Be directive. Concede to
competitors and then narrow. Cite sources by name. Reserve caveats for places
where a reader would otherwise be genuinely misled, and put them in the body
rather than the headline.

## Where craft must yield

This skill makes copy persuasive. Persuasion is what makes a false claim
dangerous, so three limits are absolute:

- **The `marketing` skill's claim rules win every time.** A catchier sentence
  that breaks one is not an improvement, it is a liability.
- **Curiosity gaps must close honestly.** Opening a gap you never close, or
  closing it with something other than what was implied, is a bait-and-switch and
  it burns the trust the whole page is trying to build.
- **Emotion must be true.** Heath's "emotional" means *make them feel what is
  actually there*. Manufactured urgency, invented scarcity and fake deadlines are
  a different trade, and not this one.

One more, wherever a real person's story is used: it is **biography**. Nothing
here authorises improving it. If a detail would land better untrue, ask the owner
for a better true detail.
